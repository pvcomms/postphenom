Fence

Before a thing is cleared away, find out what it was for. What it costs you, what it might be for and how you know, what would come through, whether it could go back up. Then the call is yours.

The claim

From where you stand, a fence whose use you cannot see looks like a fence with no use, and a reason you guessed reads like one you found out. The sheet makes you say which. It draws the difference: found in ink, guessed in pencil, a reason that no longer holds as a broken rail.

It opens on a specimen, two fences from a small studio that never existed. Start a new fence to set down your own. What you set down stays in this browser.

    what the fence holds to

    Before a fence comes down, say what it was for. The sheet asks how you know each reason — a guess, or found out by asking, reading or watching — and draws the difference: found in ink, guessed in pencil, a reason that no longer holds as a broken rail. It asks what would come through and whether it could go back up. The call is yours, and dated. Nothing here says whether a fence should come down, and nothing keeps one for being old. The one lean is Chesterton's: taking a fence down before a use is found asks once more, and the record says so.

    “If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think.”

    G. K. Chesterton, The Thing (1929)
