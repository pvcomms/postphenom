Real, Ideal

The distance between who you say you are and who you say you mean to be is a surface to walk, not a deficit to close. This sheet draws it, so you can see where it is wide, where it is narrow, and which way you have been walking.

You name a handful of dimensions in your own words, and both ends of each line. On each you write one line of the real and one of the ideal, and place them from 0 to 10. The page draws the two profiles, shades the gap between, lays your earlier sheets under as faint traces, and counts what is there. You decide what to do about one gap; later you say how it went. It opens on a specimen: three dated sheets of one person who never existed. Make your own, and what you set down stays in this browser.

    what real, ideal holds to

    You name the dimensions and both ends of every line, write the real and the ideal in your own words, and place each from 0 to 10. The page draws the two profiles to scale on your numbers, shades what lies between them, lays your earlier sheets under as faint traces, and counts: how many dimensions, which gap is widest and which narrowest by your own numbers, how many moved since the last sheet and which way, and the days. You decide what to do about one gap, and say afterwards how it went; the page dates both.

    It never says a gap is a problem, never says which to close or which way to walk, never puts one dimension above another, and never asks a model. A wide gap is a distance and a narrow one is a distance; the page measures them on your scale and leaves the weighing to you. Nothing is fetched and nothing is sent.
