Stop Flowing

Believe in something. Twelve positions, a crowd set against you, and a record of what moved.

The claim

A self put together from the feed has no keel. Its positions sit wherever the current last left them. From the inside, a position you drifted into feels exactly like one you hold. You can only tell them apart by moving the crowd and watching which ones go with it.

Gen Z is the first cohort to grow up with the crowd in the room all the time: a count under every post, a poll under every opinion, a separate audience in every app and a separate self for each. Kenneth Gergen described the condition in 1991, before any of it fit in a pocket, as a self spread across more relationships than it can hold together. Zygmunt Bauman called the period liquid. A liquid takes the shape of whatever it is poured into, and it keeps no shape of its own.

On this account, believing in something is less a feeling than a behaviour. It is a position that stays where it is when the room turns, and that you would say out loud in every room you're in. Both halves can be measured, roughly, on one person in one sitting.

IIIThe keel

The keel

What was arranged

The polls in II were generated on this page after you finished I. Each was placed on the opposite side of your first answer, with roughly four votes in five against you, and its top comment was written to argue that side. There were no voters. The six questions marked no votes yet were the control: the same questions, asked twice, with nobody in the room. One of each pair of topics got a crowd, chosen at random.

alone in the current where the crowd stood held against it

Carried means you moved toward the crowd. Pushed back means you moved away from it. Neither one held. A position that moves away from a crowd is still being placed by the crowd. Jack Brehm called the push-back reactance in 1966: the urge to take back a freedom that someone seems to be removing. The contrarian is carried by the same current, only in the other direction.

None of this is new, only the scale of it. In Solomon Asch's line-judging studies about a third of the answers on the critical trials went with a unanimous, obviously wrong majority, and about a quarter of people never went along at all. Deutsch and Gerard split the pull in two. Informational influence says they probably know something. Normative influence says you'd like them to like you. A feed runs both at once, all day. When Salganik, Dodds and Watts let 14,341 people see what others had downloaded, success in their music market became more unequal and less predictable. The best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well, but anything in between could happen. On a news site, a single planted up-vote made the next rating 32 percent more likely to be positive, and the herding lifted final ratings by a quarter. Planted down-votes got corrected.

IVThe rooms

The rooms

Holding a position is half of it. The other half is whether it comes with you. The Center's working line is that coherence between your online and offline self is the purest act of protest still available. So choose up to three of the twelve that you would defend, and say what you'd do with each in four rooms.

    0 of 3

    Alice Marwick and danah boyd called the problem of one post reaching every audience at once context collapse. The usual fix is to keep the rooms apart: an account for each audience and a version of you for each account. It works. It also means no single room ever sees the whole of you, and that includes you. The fragmentation is less in what you believe than in where you let it show.

    VArticles

    Articles

    James Marcia sorted identity by two questions: have you looked around, and have you committed? Diffusion is neither. Foreclosure is commitment without looking, a belief handed to you whole. Achievement is both. So each article asks for both at once: what you believe in your own words, where it came from, and what would move it. A belief can say what would change it. A current needs no reason to move, only a crowd.

    Coda

    "Believe in something" ran as a Nike advertising line in September 2018. The current will carry even the instruction to stand still. So the test was never whether you believe in something. It is whether the thing stays put when the room turns, and whether you would say it in every room you are in.

    Honest limits

    • Twelve positions, one sitting. The noise estimate rests on six questions, so it is rough, and the pull can come out at zero or negative by chance.
    • The title warned you and the crowd is plainly a page's crowd. Asch's participants had no warning. A warning makes people stiffer, so whatever moved here moved with your guard up.
    • You may have remembered your first answers and matched them on purpose. That counts. Choosing consistency is one of the ways a keel gets built.
    • This measures where you stand on twelve statements. Your identity is a much larger thing than that, and no page can put a number on it.
