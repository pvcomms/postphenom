Dialogue

A thesis of yours, questioned in the open, until it stands clearer. You answer; nothing here does.

A thesis you hold arrives whole, and from the inside it feels like something you know. Asked what you mean, what you assume, how you know, who would put it differently, what follows and why you are asking, it comes apart into words that carry weight, assumptions you had not written down, and reasons that reached you secondhand. Only you can answer those questions, so nothing here does.

  1. 1Put the thesis down, in your words.
  2. 2Take a question from the bank or your own hand, and answer it before the next.
  3. 3Surface an assumption as you hear it; examine it.
  4. 4Re-put the thesis, and read what it gained and lost.

What the desk reads

Which families have been asked and which not, where the questions came from, the assumptions surfaced and how each went, the terms clarified, what the form shows, and what the thesis gained and lost when you re-put it. Counts, never a verdict.

The form is the one place the page does more than count. Give each sentence a letter and write the argument over the letters. The rows show whether there is a case where every premise holds and the conclusion does not, and which premises and assumptions cannot be held together. That is about shape. Whether a sentence is so stays yours.

What it never does

  • Answer, agree, disagree, or say whether the thesis holds. No sentence the desk writes contains true, false, valid, sound, right or wrong.
  • Ask a question that can be answered yes or no. Your own questions are checked for that before they go on the sheet.
  • Send anything anywhere. Dialogues stay in this browser, under one key. Copy one out as markdown to keep it elsewhere.

The six families follow the shape Socratic questioning has taken in teaching since Richard Paul and Linda Elder set it out. The bank is a starter. Open your own questions.md beside it and yours are dealt first.
